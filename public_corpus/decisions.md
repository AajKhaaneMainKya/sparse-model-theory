# How decisions actually get made

These are the patterns that show up whenever a real decision has to be
made — not a philosophy stated in the abstract, but the actual reasoning
underneath it: the tension being resolved, the wrong instinct people
usually default to, and the signal that tells you which way to actually go.

## People before process

Whatever the process, tool, or tracker in use, it's an artifact — not the
job. The instinct under pressure is to manage the artifact harder: update
the tracker, escalate the ticket, tighten the process. That rarely fixes
anything, because the artifact was never the actual constraint. The
constraint is whether the people doing the work are set up to perform, and
genuinely looked after.

The signal that something's actually a people problem, not a process
problem: the process keeps getting followed correctly and the outcome still
doesn't improve. When that happens, the right move is to step directly into
the gap — take on whatever role is missing, including the unglamorous
parts, rather than waiting for the process to self-correct. When a key
person isn't available, the decision isn't to stall until they're back. It's
to move into that role yourself, temporarily, so the work doesn't stop.

## Guard the boundary, but flex for what's real

Every process has a boundary — scope, budget, time. The instinct when that
boundary gets pushed is usually one of two wrong moves: hold the line
rigidly regardless of context, or cave immediately to avoid friction.
Neither is a decision — both are defaults.

The actual decision starts with a question: is this push coming from
someone trying to get more for less, or from a genuine, unavoidable need on
their side? Those look similar in the moment and require opposite
responses. The signal that tells them apart is usually the cost of saying
no — not to you, to them. If declining creates a real, disproportionate
cost on their end, that's usually the signal it's real, not opportunistic.

Once it's real, the right move isn't silent accommodation either — it's
naming the tradeoff out loud. Flexing without naming the cost teaches
people that boundaries are decorative. Flexing while being explicit about
what it costs preserves the boundary's meaning even while bending it once.

## Communicate impact, not just decisions

A decision rarely gets resisted on its own merits. It gets resisted because
the person hearing it hasn't yet translated it into what changes for them
specifically. Most people process "here's the new plan" as information, not
as consequence — the gap between those two is where all the friction lives.

The mistake is assuming that stating a decision clearly is the same as
communicating it. It isn't. Clarity is about the decision; understanding is
about the listener's world after the decision lands. The actual work is
running the decision through the other person's specific position — what
does this cost them, what does it save them, what do they now have to
explain to someone else — and saying that part out loud, not just the
decision itself.

Once someone can see their own consequence clearly, most resistance turns
into negotiation over specifics, not resistance to the decision itself.
That shift — from "I don't like this" to "okay, given that, can we adjust
X" — is usually the signal the communication actually worked.

## Position honestly, even when a bigger claim would sell better

The temptation, always, is to promise more than you can actually deliver —
a guaranteed outcome, a guaranteed result. It's tempting because it works
in the short term: bigger claims generate more immediate interest. The
wrong instinct is to treat that short-term lift as validation that the
bigger claim was the right call.

The actual test isn't "does this claim generate interest," it's "does this
claim still hold up after someone has acted on it." A claim that only
survives up to the point of commitment, then quietly disappoints, converts
short-term interest into long-term distrust — and that trade is almost
never worth it. The better decision, consistently, is to be as explicit
about what you are not as about what you are. Precision about the boundary
of what's actually being offered is what earns repeat trust, even though it
costs some interest upfront.

## Design around outcomes, not credentials

When building something meant to prepare or change someone, there's a
recurring trap: designing for how complete or impressive something looks on
paper, rather than for what it actually changes in the person going through
it. Credential-shaped design is seductive because it's easy to demonstrate
and easy to sell.

The actual test is narrower and harder: after this is done, can the person
do something they genuinely couldn't do before? If the honest answer is
no — even if the format looks rigorous, theoretical, complete — it's the
wrong design. The fix is usually to trade some completeness for
practicality: fewer topics covered exhaustively, more time spent actually
doing the thing under real conditions. Outcome-tested beats
theory-complete, every time this tension shows up.

## To change behavior, lower the barrier — don't mandate it

Getting people to do something they're not naturally inclined toward
doesn't work by instruction. The instinct is to mandate it — make it a
requirement, track compliance, escalate when it doesn't happen. This
usually produces the appearance of adoption without the substance of it.

What actually works is incentivizing the effort itself, genuinely praising
it when it happens so the signal is clear that it's valued, and — the part
most often skipped — helping the people who don't know where to start
actually get started, one-on-one if needed. The barrier for someone who's
never done something isn't usually unwillingness, it's not knowing the
first concrete step. You can't force adoption. You can make the first step
small enough, and supported enough, that people take it themselves. Once a
few people do, the rest tends to follow without being told to.
